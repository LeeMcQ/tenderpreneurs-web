Pricing a government tender is part science, part strategy, and entirely consequential. Price too high and you lose on points. Price too low and you either lose margin, fail to perform, or — in worst cases — trigger an abnormally low tender investigation.

This guide walks South African SMMEs through a structured approach to tender pricing that wins business without sacrificing financial sustainability.

## Understanding How Price Translates to Points

Under the PPPFA preference point system, price determines 80% (or 90%) of your total score. The scoring formula rewards the lowest valid bidder with maximum points, and penalises higher bids proportionally:

```
Price Score = 80 × (1 – (Your Price – Lowest Valid Price) / Lowest Valid Price)
```

This means:
- Being 5% above the lowest bid = you lose **4 price points** (out of 80)
- Being 10% above = you lose **8 points**
- Being 20% above = you lose **16 points**

With a strong B-BBEE position (Level 1 = 20 points under 80/20), you can absorb some price premium and still win. With a weaker B-BBEE position, price competitiveness becomes critical.

**The strategic implication:** On tenders where your B-BBEE advantage is 12–20 points, you can afford to price at market rate and compete. On tenders where everyone has similar B-BBEE scores, winning becomes a pricing game.

## Step 1: Build Your Cost Model from the Ground Up

Never start with "what do I think I can charge?" Start with "what does it actually cost to deliver this?"

### Labour Costs

Government tenders often involve detailed staffing plans. For each person involved in the contract:

1. **Gross salary / contractor day rate** — the base cost
2. **Employer contributions** — add 15–20% for UIF (1%), SDL (1%), employer PAYE, and any medical aid contribution
3. **Leave provision** — 15 days annual leave + 12 public holidays = approximately 11.5% of working days
4. **Management overhead** — how much of your directors'/managers' time will this contract consume?
5. **Fringe benefits** — vehicle allowance, phone, laptop depreciation

**Loaded labour cost** = Gross × 1.35–1.45 for most SA small businesses

### Material and Direct Costs

For goods or construction:
- **Actual supplier quotes** — never estimate materials; get quotes
- **Wastage factor** — materials pricing typically includes 5–10% wastage
- **Delivery and transport** — often forgotten, frequently significant
- **Storage** — if you need to warehouse materials before deployment
- **Import duties** — relevant for equipment or specialist components

### Overhead Allocation

Your overhead costs exist regardless of whether this tender runs. You must recover them:

- Rent / bond payments (office, workshop, yard)
- Utilities (electricity, water)
- Insurance (public liability, vehicle, equipment)
- Accounting and legal fees
- Vehicle costs (depreciation, fuel, maintenance, insurance)
- IT and telecoms
- Marketing and bid preparation costs

Divide your total annual overhead by the number of contracts you run annually to get a per-contract overhead allocation, or allocate overhead as a percentage of contract value (typically 10–20% for service-based SMMEs).

### Profit Margin

This is not optional. A sustainable SMME needs to return profit. Recommended target ranges for government work:

- **Services (professional, consulting):** 15–25% net margin
- **Goods supply:** 10–18% net margin
- **Construction:** 8–15% net margin (tighter due to risk and capital requirements)
- **Specialist or proprietary work:** 20–35% (where you have unique capability)

If your calculated cost + overhead + target margin puts you 30% above market, the problem is not your margin. The problem is your cost structure or that this tender is not right for your business.

## Step 2: Understand the Market Rate

Unlike private sector tenders where pricing is entirely confidential, government tender outcomes are increasingly public. Use these sources:

### Historical tender awards

The **etenders.gov.za** portal publishes tender award notices including the awarded price. Search for similar contracts in the same sector, province, and value range from the past 2–3 years. These give you a benchmark.

**Adjustments for inflation:**
- CPI in South Africa has averaged 4–6% in recent years
- For a contract awarded 2 years ago, adjust historical prices upward by approximately 8–12%
- Use the Stats SA CPI index for specific cost categories (e.g., Construction Cost Index, PPI for materials)

### SARS binding general rulings

Where your pricing includes VAT, be precise. Government buyers understand zero-rated vs standard-rated vs exempt supplies. Mismatch in VAT treatment vs what's quoted can create disputes post-award.

### Industry rate cards

Professional associations often publish rate cards:
- Engineering Council (ECSA) published engineering tariffs
- South African Institute of Architects fee guidelines
- Association for Project Management fee norms

These are starting points, not ceilings.

## Step 3: Understand Abnormally Low Tenders

South African procurement law recognises that bids priced unrealistically low create delivery risk. **PFMA Regulation 16A6.4** and the SCM Policy Framework allow accounting officers to query bids where the price is so low that the contract appears undeliverable.

An abnormally low tender (ALT) investigation can lead to:
- Request for detailed cost breakdown justification
- Conditional award requiring a performance guarantee
- Rejection of the bid

Signs your pricing may trigger an ALT query:
- Your price is more than 25–30% below the next lowest bid
- Your price appears to exclude statutory costs (VAT, UIF, SDL)
- Your price does not cover the specified minimum wage for the duration of the contract

**Never price below cost to win a tender.** The consequences extend beyond financial loss — a defaulted contract damages your reputation with that organ of state and may appear in reference checks for future bids.

## Step 4: The Competitive Positioning Matrix

Before finalising your price, position yourself on this matrix:

| | High B-BBEE Advantage | Low B-BBEE Advantage |
|---|---|---|
| **High Confidence in Delivery** | Price at or slightly above market | Price at or below market |
| **Moderate Delivery Confidence** | Price at market | Price slightly below market |
| **Low Delivery Confidence** | Do not bid | Do not bid |

Only bid where you are in the top half of this matrix.

## Step 5: Protect Against Cost Escalation

Government contracts typically run 1–3 years. Your costs will increase. If you don't protect for escalation, you will be delivering at a loss by year 2.

### Escalation clauses

Bid documents may already provide for an escalation mechanism — check the contract terms. Common approaches:
- **CPI-linked:** Annual price increase = CPI for prior 12 months
- **Fixed percentage:** e.g., 5% per year
- **Material-specific indices:** For construction, the agreed indices for specific cost components

If the tender does not include an escalation clause and the contract is multi-year, you must build expected inflation into your year 1 price.

**Rule of thumb:** For a 3-year contract with no escalation clause, price as if Year 1 = 110% of your actual Year 1 cost, Year 2 = 120%, Year 3 = 130% — then average these for a single annual rate.

### Contingency

Build a contingency provision into every bid — typically 5–10% of total project cost. This is your buffer for:
- Scope interpretations that differ from your assumption
- Site conditions (construction)
- Additional government reporting requirements
- Staff turnover during the contract

Do not label contingency separately in your price schedule — it should be embedded in your unit rates and totals.

## Step 6: Structuring Your Bill of Quantities

Government bids typically require pricing in a specific format. Common structures:

### Fixed-price lump sum
One price for the entire scope. Low flexibility, high price certainty. Better for well-defined scopes.

### Schedule of rates / unit price
Price per measurable unit (per hour, per kg, per km, per item). Government pays based on actual quantities delivered. Better for variable or uncertain scopes. Requires careful unit rate construction.

### Cost-plus (rare in government)
Actual cost + agreed fee/margin. Highest financial risk for government; used mainly for highly complex or innovative projects.

For schedule of rates contracts, your **unit rates** must be internally consistent. Evaluators and auditors compare rates to market benchmarks. An anomalously high unit rate for a high-volume item (to compensate for underpriced low-volume items) is an unbalanced bid — this can be grounds for rejection.

## Step 7: The Final Price Review

Before submitting your pricing schedule:

1. **Add VAT correctly.** Standard rate is 15%. Confirm which line items are VAT-applicable and which (if any) are zero-rated.

2. **Check arithmetic.** Every extension (quantity × unit rate) and every total must be arithmetically correct. Calculation errors can be corrected administratively, but they create doubt about your competence.

3. **Compare to your cost model.** Your submitted price must exceed your total estimated cost (including overhead and profit). If it does not, your cost model has an error.

4. **Cross-check against available budget.** Most tenders include the available budget or a broad price envelope in the bid documents. If your price significantly exceeds the available budget, you will not win. Significantly below = abnormally low tender risk.

5. **Review price reasonableness.** Ask: if I were the accounting officer evaluating this bid, would this price seem reasonable for the scope? Trust your intuition here.
