The Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), Act 1 of 1999, is the cornerstone of financial governance in South Africa's national and provincial government spheres. For procurement professionals, PFMA compliance is not a box-ticking exercise â€” it is a legal obligation that shapes every stage of the supply chain. The Act regulates how public funds are spent, ensuring transparency, accountability, and value for money.

## The core sections

At its core, PFMA compliance means following the prescripts of sections 38, 51, and 76.

**Section 38** places general financial management responsibilities on accounting officers, requiring them to maintain effective procurement and provisioning systems that are fair, equitable, transparent, competitive, and cost-effective.

**Section 51** extends similar duties to the boards of public entities.

**Section 76** outlines the framework for the National Treasury's regulations, including the detailed supply chain management rules found in the Treasury Regulations.

## What it means in daily operations

For a procurement officer, compliance touches daily operations. Every request for quotation or tender above R30 000 (including VAT) must be underpinned by proper demand planning, documented cost estimates, and an evaluation process that withstands audit scrutiny. The PFMA mandates that goods and services are procured only through authorised supply chain management policies, backed by valid budget allocations.

A procurement decision that ignores these steps exposes the institution to irregular expenditure â€” a finding that must be reported to the Auditor-General and can lead to disciplinary action against the responsible official.

## Treasury Instruction Notes

One of the most misunderstood aspects is the link between the PFMA and National Treasury Instruction Notes. These notes update thresholds and procedures, such as the mandatory use of the Central Supplier Database (CSD) and eTender portal. Failing to comply with an instruction note carries the same weight as breaching a regulation.

In recent years, Treasury has tightened the rules on deviations, expansions of scope, and contracts that bypass competitive processes. Procurement professionals must document the justification for any deviation and obtain the correct authority before proceeding.

## Compliance after the award

Post-award, PFMA compliance does not end. Contract management, performance monitoring, and timely payments are all part of the compliance chain. The Act requires that accounting officers report quarterly on all tenders awarded, including the names of successful bidders, amounts, and B-BBEE levels.

In addition, the PFMA's materiality and loss control framework means any suspected fraud, theft, or gross negligence in procurement must be reported immediately to the National Treasury and the South African Police Service.

## Consequences of non-compliance

Non-compliance carries severe consequences. An accounting officer who wilfully or negligently makes an unauthorised expenditure may be charged financially. The institution may incur an audit qualification, damaging its reputation and blocking future funding. For bidders, doing business with a non-compliant department is risky; contracts can be set aside, and payment runs delayed indefinitely.

Ultimately, PFMA compliance builds a procurement ecosystem where public money is protected. It is the lens through which every specification, evaluation score, and contract variation must be viewed. When procurement professionals internalise its principles, they not only safeguard their institutions but also contribute to a clean administration that serves South African citizens with integrity.
