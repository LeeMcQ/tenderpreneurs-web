TITLE: What Is PFMA Compliance and Why It Matters in Public Procurement
BODY:
The Public Finance Management Act (PFMA), Act 1 of 1999, is the cornerstone of financial governance in South Africa’s national and provincial government spheres. For procurement professionals, PFMA compliance is not a box-ticking exercise—it is a legal obligation that shapes every stage of the supply chain. The Act regulates how public funds are spent, ensuring transparency, accountability, and value for money.

At its core, PFMA compliance means following the prescripts of sections 38, 51, and 76. Section 38 places general financial management responsibilities on accounting officers, requiring them to maintain effective procurement and provisioning systems that are fair, equitable, transparent, competitive, and cost-effective. Section 51 extends similar duties to the boards of public entities, while Section 76 outlines the framework for the National Treasury’s regulations, including the detailed supply chain management rules found in the Treasury Regulations.

For a procurement officer, compliance touches daily operations. Every request for quotation or tender above R30 000 (including VAT) must be underpinned by proper demand planning, documented cost estimates, and an evaluation process that withstands audit scrutiny. The PFMA mandates that goods and services are procured only through authorised supply chain management policies, backed by valid budget allocations. A procurement decision that ignores these steps exposes the institution to irregular expenditure—a finding that must be reported to the Auditor-General and can lead to disciplinary action against the responsible official.

One of the most misunderstood aspects is the link between the PFMA and National Treasury Instruction Notes. These notes update thresholds and procedures, such as the mandatory use of the Central Supplier Database (CSD) and eTender portal. Failing to comply with an instruction note carries the same weight as breaching a regulation. In recent years, Treasury has tightened the rules on deviations, expansions of scope, and contracts that bypass competitive processes. Procurement professionals must document the justification for any deviation and obtain the correct authority before proceeding.

Post-award, PFMA compliance does not end. Contract management, performance monitoring, and timely payments are all part of the compliance chain. The Act requires that accounting officers report quarterly on all tenders awarded, including the names of successful bidders, amounts, and B-BBEE levels. In addition, the PFMA’s materiality and loss control framework means any suspected fraud, theft, or gross negligence in procurement must be reported immediately to the National Treasury and the South African Police Service.

Non-compliance carries severe consequences. An accounting officer who wilfully or negligently makes an unauthorised expenditure may be charged financially. The institution may incur an audit qualification, damaging its reputation and blocking future funding. For bidders, doing business with a non-compliant department is risky; contracts can be set aside, and payment runs delayed indefinitely.

Ultimately, PFMA compliance builds a procurement ecosystem where public money is protected. It is the lens through which every specification, evaluation score, and contract variation must be viewed. When procurement professionals internalise its principles, they not only safeguard their institutions but also contribute to a clean administration that serves South African citizens with integrity.

TITLE: How to Register on the CSD: A Practical Walkthrough for Suppliers
BODY:
The Central Supplier Database (CSD) is the single source of supplier information for all spheres of government in South Africa. Established by the National Treasury, it eliminates the need for businesses to submit the same compliance documents to every organ of state. For any company seeking to do business with the public sector, a complete and validated CSD registration is non-negotiable.

The process begins online at www.csd.gov.za. You need a device with internet access and an active email address. Start by clicking “Supplier Registration” and completing the self-registration form. The system requires basic details: company name, trading name (if different), type of supplier (individual, sole proprietor, partnership, or company), and the enterprise’s legal registration number. For companies and close corporations, this means the CK or enterprise number from the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC). All information must match the CIPC register exactly; discrepancies will cause validation failures.

Next, you will enter tax compliance data. The CSD interfaces directly with the South African Revenue Service (SARS). You must provide your income tax reference number and ensure the tax type and number align with SARS records. If the business holds a valid Tax Compliance Status PIN, the system will pull the status automatically. Note that the CSD does not replace the need for a tax clearance certificate during tender submission—it merely verifies that the tax number exists and the entity’s status is visible to government.

The third critical step is uploading the company’s B-BBEE certificate or sworn affidavit. The database accepts a verified B-BBEE certificate issued by a SANAS-accredited verification agency, or an affidavit for exempted micro enterprises (EMEs) and qualifying small enterprises (QSEs). The system reads the certificate number and expiry date. If your B-BBEE certificate is older than 12 months, or if you upload a handwritten affidavit that is not commissioned properly, the validation will stall. Always scan clear, colour images of the original documents.

Banking details form another pillar of the registration. You must provide the bank name, account number, branch code, and account type. Importantly, the CSD now requires that the bank account be verified via the Bank Account Verification (BAV) service embedded in the system. This step confirms that the account belongs to the supplier and prevents fraud. If the bank verification fails, the CSD record will remain incomplete. It is essential to ensure the account is in the name of the registered supplier and is active.

After all sections are filled, submit the registration. The CSD issues a unique supplier number and a summary report immediately. However, “registered” status does not mean “compliance checked.” The National Treasury validation team reviews the data against CIPC, SARS, and bank records. A status will show as “Compliant,” “Not Compliant,” or “Verification Pending.” Suppliers should not bid with a non-compliant status—organs of state cannot award contracts to an invalid CSD record.

Common mistakes include outdated CIPC annual returns, dormant SARS profiles, and mismatched physical addresses. Keep your CSD profile updated. Any change to company details, B-BBEE level, or banking information must be amended promptly. Invalid data can trigger rejection during tender evaluations. By treating the CSD as a living profile and not a once-off task, businesses position themselves to participate in public sector opportunities seamlessly and remain compliant throughout the contract lifecycle.

TITLE: Understanding the 80/20 PPPFA Rule in Government Tenders
BODY:
The Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act (PPPFA) and its 2022 regulations govern how organs of state evaluate tenders using a points system that balances price and socio-economic objectives. The most widely applied formula is the 80/20 preference point system, used for tenders with a value below R50 million (including VAT). Understanding this rule is essential for procurement officials drafting evaluation criteria and for bidders structuring their offers.

Under the 80/20 system, a total of 100 points is allocated. Up to 80 points are reserved for price, and a maximum of 20 points may be awarded for specific goals linked to the tender. Price points are calculated using a relative scoring formula: the lowest acceptable bid gets the full 80 points, while other bids receive points proportionally. For example, if Bid A quotes R1 million and Bid B quotes R1.2 million, Bid A scores 80, and Bid B scores (1 000 000 / 1 200 000) × 80 = 66.67 points. Accuracy in pricing and completeness of the financial offer is therefore paramount.

The remaining 20 points are not automatic. They are allocated only if the bidder attains stated specific goals, which the organ of state must clearly articulate in the tender documents. The 2022 regulations include mandatory goals: contracting with companies that are at least 51% black-owned, 30% black-woman-owned, 30% black-youth-owned, or 7% black-people-with-disabilities-owned. Organs of state may subdivide the 20 points among these categories and, where justifiable, add local content or other socio-economic objectives. If a bidder does not meet any goal, they score zero out of 20, regardless of price.

A critical nuance is that the 80/20 rule applies only to tenders above R2 000 and below R50 million. For contracts of R50 million or above, the 90/10 system kicks in. Furthermore, the regulations tighten the link between B-BBEE and preference points. An organ of state may not award preference points solely on the basis of a B-BBEE level certificate unless the tender specifically applies the B-BBEE dimension as one of the measurable specific goals. The contract must stipulate how B-BBEE status contributes to the 20 points, and the bidder must submit a valid certificate or sworn affidavit to claim those points.

Calculating the final score is straightforward: price points out of 80 plus specific goal points out of 20. The tender must be awarded to the bidder with the highest total points, unless objective criteria justify otherwise. However, the regulations also demand that the winning bid passes the functionality threshold. If a tender includes functionality criteria, only bids scoring above a minimum quality hurdle proceed to the preference point stage. This prevents a pure race to the bottom on price.

Procurement practitioners must document the evaluation of specific goals meticulously. The score sheet must reflect how each bidder’s claimed goals were verified, whether through the CSD B-BBEE indicator, affidavits, or other evidence. Any mathematical error in price scoring can be a source of dispute, so independent checking is advisable. For bidders, the lesson is to study the tender’s specific goal matrix and assemble all supporting proofs before submission. A missing signed affidavit could mean forfeiting up to 20 points—the difference between winning and losing.

TITLE: B-BBEE Levels Explained for Tenders: What Counts and How to Prove It
BODY:
Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment (B-BBEE) is a central pillar of South Africa’s public procurement system. The B-BBEE Act and the Amended Codes of Good Practice define how entities are measured on ownership, management control, skills development, enterprise and supplier development, and socio-economic development. The result is a B-BBEE recognition level, which directly influences a company’s competitiveness in government tenders.

Currently, the generic scorecard produces eight recognition levels, with Level 1 being the highest. A Level 1 contributor scores 100 points or more on the scorecard and is recognised as a 135% B-BBEE procurement contributor. A Level 8 contributor scores between 40 and 55 points and receives a 10% recognition. Exempted Micro Enterprises (EMEs) with annual turnover under R10 million automatically qualify as Level 1 if they are 51% or more black-owned, or Level 4 if less than 51% black-owned. Qualifying Small Enterprises (QSEs) with turnover between R10 million and R50 million must undergo a verification or may rely on an affidavit for certain elements if they are 51% black-owned.

For tenders, these levels translate into preference points only when the specific goals of the tender include B-BBEE as a measurable criterion. Under the 80/20 system, a tender may allocate a portion of the 20 points to B-BBEE level. Typically, a Level 1 might earn the full sub-allocation, Level 2 slightly less, scaling down to Level 8 or non-compliant status scoring zero. In construction tenders, the Construction Sector Code applies, which modifies the elements and thresholds slightly, but the recognition level framework remains consistent.

Proof of B-BBEE status is required at bid submission. For EMEs and QSEs relying on the automatic level, a sworn affidavit or a certificate issued by the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC) via the B-BBEE portal is acceptable. Larger entities must supply a verified B-BBEE certificate issued by a SANAS-accredited verification agency. The certificate or affidavit must be signed by an authorised representative and be less than 12 months old. An expired certificate, or one that does not state the recognition level clearly, renders the bid non-responsive if B-BBEE points were claimed.

Bid committees must verify the submitted B-BBEE information against the CSD, where the supplier’s certificate is stored. Discrepancies between the physical certificate and the CSD record can delay an award or lead to disqualification. It is therefore vital for bidders to upload the identical, valid certificate to their CSD profile. Furthermore, misrepresenting B-BBEE status is a criminal offence under the B-BBEE Act, punishable by fines, imprisonment, and exclusion from doing business with the state for up to 10 years.

Strategic bidders often structure joint ventures or sub-contracting arrangements to improve the overall B-BBEE contribution of a proposal. The amended regulations allow measured entities to claim enhanced recognition if they contract with exempted micro enterprises or black-owned QSEs in the supply chain. For procurement professionals, understanding the interplay between B-BBEE levels and the PPPFA specific goals matrix is essential; the line between drafting a compliant evaluation schedule and one that inadvertently limits competition can be thin. Transparency, consistency, and a rigorous verification protocol protect both the organ of state and genuine bidders in a procurement landscape where empowerment remains a constitutional imperative.

If provisional sums or contingencies are included, treat them according to the instructions. For tenders with 80/20 preference points, your price determines up to 80 points, so ensure your offer is competitive but sustainable. An abnormally low bid can be investigated and rejected if you cannot justify it.

Before sealing the envelope or uploading digitally, get a second pair of eyes to check the compliance checklist. It is easy to miss a signature or a certified copy. Deliver the bid on time—late bids are returned unopened. Then, preserve a complete copy of your submission for your records. A compliant bid is a mirror image of the tender’s requirements, nothing more, nothing less. By making compliance your first and last check, you ensure your company’s ideas and expertise are actually evaluated.

TITLE: SBD Forms Checklist: Your Go-To Guide for Bid Documentation
BODY:
Standard Bidding Documents (SBD) are the administrative glue of public procurement in South Africa. They are prescribed by the National Treasury to standardise the declarations, commitments, and certifications that bidders must submit. A single missing or incorrectly completed SBD form can invalidate an otherwise excellent bid. Here is a practical checklist of the core SBD forms and what procurement professionals and bidders must know about each.

SBD 1: Invitation to Bid. This is the cover page. It captures the bid number, description, closing date and time, and bidder’s details. Check that the address, contact information, and CSD supplier number are correctly filled in. The bidder must sign the declaration that all information is true and that they accept the conditions of tender.
SBD 2: Tax Matters. This form requires the bidder to declare their tax compliance status. Attach the SARS-issued Tax Compliance Status PIN or the TCS certificate. The CSD report must mirror this status. Ensure the tax reference number matches SARS records exactly.
SBD 3.1: Pricing Schedule – Firm Prices. For goods or services delivered within a short period, this schedule captures the prices that will remain firm for the contract duration. Any deviation from the format, such as adding rows or omitting unit prices, can be grounds for non-responsiveness.
SBD 3.2: Pricing Schedule – Price Adjustments. Used for longer-term contracts where CPI or other index-based adjustments are allowed. Bidders must specify the basis of the escalation formula. If the tender does not permit escalation, submitting this form instead of SBD 3.1 invalidates the bid.
SBD 4: Declaration of Interest. This is critical for transparency. Bidders must disclose whether any director, member, or employee is connected to a person employed by the state, including public service employees, members of parliament, or municipal councillors. Non-disclosure of a family relationship can lead to disqualification and possible criminal referral.
SBD 5: National Education, Training and Development Policy. requires a declaration that the bidder is not listed on the National Treasury’s register of tender defaulters or restricted suppliers. It also asks questions about the bidder’s involvement in any collusive tendering practices.
SBD 6.1: Preference Points Claim Form – 80/20. This form must be completed for tenders below R50 million. Bidders indicate their B-BBEE level and attach supporting proof. The form calculates the points claimed. The evaluators will verify the claim against the attached certificate or affidavit.
SBD 6.2: Preference Points Claim Form – 90/10. Equivalent for tenders of R50 million and above. The same verification rules apply.
SBD 7.2: Contract Form – Rendering of Services. Once a bid is accepted, this becomes the binding contract. At bid stage, it must be signed as an acceptance of the proposed terms.
SBD 8: Declaration of Bidder’s Past Supply Chain Management Practices. requires disclosure of any prior contract cancellations, liquidated damages imposed, or litigation with organs of state. This is not to penalise but to assess risk.
SBD 9: Certificate of Independent Bid Determination. Bidders certify they arrived at the bid independently, without collusion. False certification can result in criminal prosecution.

Beyond these, additional SBDs like SBD 6.3 (for functionality) and schedules for local content may appear. Always check the bid’s returnable schedule; if an SBD is listed, it is mandatory. Use the latest versions from the eTender portal or the departmental website. Bidders should store blank, up-to-date SBD sets and ensure every field is completed. A well-maintained SBD checklist transforms panic at the deadline into quiet confidence.

TITLE: What Makes a Tender Non-Responsive: The Pitfalls to Avoid
BODY:
In South African public procurement, a non-responsive tender is one that fails to meet the mandatory requirements set out in the tender documents. It is excluded from further evaluation, regardless of how innovative or cheap it might be. Understanding what triggers non-responsiveness saves bidders money and protects procurement officials from costly review challenges.

The most common cause is late submission. The closing date and time are absolute. A bid that arrives one minute after the deadline is non-responsive and must be returned unopened. This rule is strict; even traffic delays or courier errors do not excuse lateness. Next is non-attendance of a compulsory briefing session. If the tender documents state that attendance is mandatory and the bidder’s representative did not sign the attendance register at the specified venue and time, the bid falls away.

Administrative non-responsiveness stems from missing or incorrectly completed returnable documents. If the SBD 1 is unsigned, or the CSD summary report is not attached and the supplier number is not reflected, evaluators cannot verify the bidder’s registration. A missing tax compliance pin or an expired B-BBEE certificate linked to claimed preference points will also render the bid non-responsive. These are objective failures, not matters of discretion.

Technical non-responsiveness occurs when the bidder fails to meet the stated minimum functionality threshold. Suppose a tender requires five years’ experience in civil works and the bidder submits proof of only three years. If the cut-off is absolute, the bid is rejected. Similarly, if a tender mandates that key personnel hold specific professional registrations (e.g., PrEng for an engineer) and the bidder’s candidate is not registered, the bid fails. The evaluation committee has no mandate to condone a deviation.

Pricing-related non-responsiveness often relates to incomplete or altered price schedules. If the bidder adjusts the quantities in the bill of quantities, or omits a line item entirely, the whole tender may be rejected. An ambiguous price, such as writing “to be negotiated,” is fatal. Bids that exceed the approved budget, if a budget cap is disclosed, are also non-responsive. Recently, Treasury has clarified that an abnormally low bid can be declared non-responsive if the bidder cannot provide a satisfactory written explanation of its cost structure.

Non-compliance with local content and designation regulations is a growing reason for non-responsiveness. For sectors like steel, furniture, or electrical equipment, bidders must use SBD 6.2 and detailed local content schedules to demonstrate that the goods exceed the minimum local content threshold. An incorrectly completed certificate, or a simple omission of the supporting spreadsheet, leads to disqualification.

Unfair procurement consequences follow both ways: evaluators who wrongly declare a responsive bid non-responsive can face a tender review application. Conversely, accepting a non-responsive bid constitutes an irregular expenditure. Therefore, bid specifications must clearly differentiate between mandatory (non-waivable) requirements and desirable criteria. Words like “must” and “shall” signal mandatory conditions; “may” or “should” indicate desirable ones. For bidders, the rule is simple: treat every “must” in the document as an absolute instruction. A systematic compliance matrix prepared before submission can be the difference between a winning bid and the frustration of a non-responsive letter.

TITLE: How Win Probability Is Calculated: A Tenderer’s Guide to Scoring
BODY:
Winning a government tender is often seen as a lottery, but smart bidders calculate their win probability before committing resources. Win probability estimation combines a realistic assessment of the competition, analysis of the evaluation criteria, and honest introspection about the company’s strengths and gaps. It is both an art and a science critical for go/no-go decisions.

The first step is to dissect the evaluation criteria and their weightings. In a typical 80/20 tender with functionality, the total points are split: a minimum functionality threshold must be passed, then 80 points for price and 20 for specific goals. Functionality is often scored out of 100, with a threshold of, say, 70 points. If your own honest self-assessment against the published scoring matrix gives you only 65 points, your win probability is zero, irrespective of price. That saves you the cost of preparing a full bid.

If you clear functionality, estimate your likely price score. Gather intelligence on your competitors’ likely pricing. If you have bid against the same firms before, their historical margins and price models are a reference point. Assume the lowest bidder will set the benchmark and earn 80 points. If your estimate is 15% higher than that forecast lowest price, your price score will be roughly (Lowest / Yours) × 80. For instance, if the expected lowest bid is R2 million and your price is R2.5 million, your price score is (2/2.5) × 80 = 64 points.

Next, calculate your specific goal points. Examine the matrix closely. If the tender allocates 10 points to B-BBEE Level 1 and you hold a Level 2, you might earn 9 points. If another 5 points go to locality and you are from within the municipal area, you secure 5. If 5 points go to women ownership and you qualify, you get 5. This earns you a total of 19 out of 20. A competitor with a Level 1 and similar locality might get the full 20. Enter these into a simple spreadsheet.

Combine the numbers: functionality (pass), price score (64), specific goals (19) gives a total of 83 points out of 100. Now you must place this in a competitive field. You do not know the exact number of bidders, but you can estimate how many capable firms will respond. If you think there are three competitors who can beat your price and match your specific goals, your win probability would be lower than 25%. But if only one competitor has a clear price advantage, you might still win if your specific goals edge them out.

Sophisticated bidders use weighted decision matrices and even Monte Carlo simulation where the spread of possible lowest price is modelled. However, at practical level, the biggest factor is often disqualification. If your bid is fully compliant and others commonly make errors, your actual win probability rises. Research shows that in many government tenders, 20–30% of bids are non-responsive. Factoring in this “compliance attrition” can boost your calculated odds.

Win probability is not a guarantee, but it drives discipline. If the figure is below 15%, consider whether the tender is a strategic reference or a profit-making opportunity. Use it to focus on tenders where your company’s profile aligns tightly with the evaluation criteria, and where the price gap is bridgeable. Calculated bidding turns procurement from a gamble into a portfolio of measured risks.

TITLE: Common Reasons Bids Are Disqualified: Bidders’ Top Mistakes and How to Fix Them
BODY:
Every year, hundreds of millions of rands in government contracts are awarded—or not—based on compliance. Disqualification is the fastest way to waste the investment of preparing a bid. The National Treasury and provincial audit outcomes consistently highlight the same cluster of fatal mistakes. If you can eliminate these, you dramatically increase your success rate.

1. Late submission. This tops the list. A bid delivered after the specified closing time must be returned unopened. Logistics failures—traffic, courier delays, or misreading the deadline—carry zero weight. Always aim to submit the day before, and if possible, have a backup person ready to hand-deliver.

2. Non-attendance of compulsory briefings. If the tender advert says “compulsory,” it is not negotiable. Bidders who fail to sign the attendance register at the exact time and venue are disqualified. Sometimes briefings are held at remote project sites; plan travel with a large buffer.

3. Missing or invalid tax compliance verification. A bidder must produce a SARS Tax Compliance Status PIN and reflect a “Compliant” status on the CSD. If the PIN is invalid, expired, or the tax matters are not in order at the time of evaluation, the bid is out.

4. Expired or improperly substantiated B-BBEE certificates. Bidders claiming preference points for B-BBEE must attach a valid (less than 12 months old) certificate or sworn affidavit. A certificate that does not match the CSD record, or an uncertified copy, will lead to the points being zeroed, and if points were essential, the bid may be declared non-responsive.

5. Incomplete SBD forms. SBD 4 (declaration of interest), SBD 8 (past SCM practices), and SBD 9 (independent bid determination) are often overlooked. Blank spaces or “N/A” where disclosure is required can be interpreted as non-disclosure. Fill every field honestly, and sign every required signature.

6. Omitted or incorrect pricing schedules. Any alteration to the bill of quantities, omission of a line item, or a conditional offer (“price valid for 10 days instead of the required 90 days”) disqualifies the bid. Use the exact spreadsheet provided; do not hide formulas.

7. Failure to meet minimum functionality. If the tender requires three years of experience and the bidder demonstrates only two, the evaluators have a blank duty to disqualify. No amount of explanation post-submission can remedy it.

8. CSD non-compliance. A supplier number that is not validated by the National Treasury or has discrepancies with the bidding entity’s details causes instant disqualification. Keep your CSD profile updated and compliant.

9. Lack of required samples or demonstrations. Some tenders for goods require samples to be submitted by a certain date. Missing the drop-off deadline sinks the bid.

10. Misrepresentation and fraud. Any material misrepresentation, whether about B-BBEE, past performance, or qualifications, leads to disqualification and referral to the authorities for prosecution. Transparency is the only safe path.

A disciplined internal review using a compliance checklist, based on the tender’s returnable schedule, catches most of these errors. Procure a second, independent reviewer—fresh eyes see what stressed bid teams miss. In a space where margins are tight, simply not being disqualified is a competitive advantage.
